# Follow-up research recalibrates the Dot Society plan

The follow-up research leaves the Dot Society's architecture intact but changes many of its numbers and three of its methods. Real policing is much sparser and less certain than the plan assumes. The United States has **2.3 sworn officers per 1,000 residents**, 4–13 times sparser than the plan's 1–3% police share, and only about **4% of thefts end in a clearance**. The plan's Epstein formula, by contrast, makes an offender facing one officer perceive a 90% risk of arrest, and the plan never checks how often arrests actually happen. The plan's validation rule, "holds in most of 20 seeds", cannot certify the small effects a calibrated city produces, such as the **1.4% drop in theft** that 10% more police buys. Three engineering choices also need to change. Chrome 150 adopted a new math library in June 2026, so any `Math.*` call left in the core, such as the plan's Lengnick power function, makes replays differ between Chrome versions and operating systems. Dedicated workers keep running in hidden desktop tabs, so the simulation will drain batteries unless the page pauses it. And the plan should settle on the raw WebGL2 renderer its M0 already specifies: about 1 KB hand-written, against 110–157 KB gzip for the PixiJS its summary recommends, which has an open iOS context-loss bug. No project yet combines all five ingredients, but the gap has narrowed to a merger. economySim has the planned typed-array, integer-cent architecture without crime; SocSim has tax-funded enforcers chasing thieves without a market; and ndouglas/SugarScape now models rule-based theft and witnessing. The plan's most original feature, the true-versus-recorded crime view, gains a concrete headline scenario and an ethical frame. Two districts have identical true crime but different reporting rates, a predictor is trained on records, the dots carry no demographic traits, and the reader bets on the outcome before pressing Run. One caveat runs through everything: the network proxy blocked most primary sources, so many statistics below come from search summaries and need checking before they are hard-coded.

## Two near-twins appeared, but nobody has merged crime with a market

The first round's central claim still holds, by a narrower margin. GitHub was searched through its public search API, which showed only the top 15–18 results per query. Hacker News and itch.io were searched only through search snippets, and Reddit and Steam not at all. No project found combines browser-only hosting, rule-based agents, a live dot view, police, thief and merchant roles, and a market economy. What the search did find is two hobby projects that each cover one half of the idea, both MIT-licensed and both with zero stars.

**AtakanAytar/economySim** is an architectural near-twin of the plan ([GitHub](https://github.com/AtakanAytar/economySim)). It simulates about 25,000 individual people and firms in TypeScript struct-of-arrays typed arrays, with a ledger in which "money is conserved to the exact cent" and seeded, bit-identical runs backed by 77 tests. It covers goods, labour, housing, banking and a Taylor-rule central bank, all drawn on a Canvas2D city map. Its author left crime out on purpose, because it is "only interesting against a rich 'normal'" and "thin and additive on flows the ledger already double-entries". That independently supports the plan's order of economy first, crime second. The crime layer economySim plans, money laundering watched by a regulator trying to detect it, is a true-versus-recorded frame applied to finance.

**pietro-works/SocSim** covers the other half ([GitHub](https://github.com/pietro-works/SocSim)). It has "a few hundred square creatures", among them Enforcers "paid out of taxes" who chase Predators that steal and build hideouts, with sixteen sliders and speeds up to 1000×. But it has no money, prices or merchants, and crime is a fixed type of creature rather than an action any creature can take. Several smaller projects add details worth borrowing.

The bigger change is to **ndouglas/SugarScape**. Between 25 September and 3 October 2026 it grew into a playground of more than 25 classic artificial-society models, still with zero stars and with 1,093 commits since its creation on 22 September ([GitHub](https://github.com/ndouglas/SugarScape)). Its Minds 6 model lets agents steal one another's buried food, and "reciprocal pilfering then arises; it isn't scripted". Minds 8 adds watching: an agent who sees another bury food remembers the spot and returns to take it, while "the owner never knows it was seen". Together they form a working rule-based model of opportunistic and witnessed theft, close to the plan's design of crime as an action plus witnesses. A commit on 3 October mentions an upcoming "underworld" campaign whose content is unknown ([commits](https://github.com/ndouglas/SugarScape/commits/main)). ndouglas is still a set of separate models rather than one society where merchants and police coexist. But a merger of its pieces, or of economySim's ledger with SocSim's enforcers, is now the competitor to watch.

ndouglas's interface is worth copying directly. Compare mode runs two worlds of one model in lockstep. A seek bar steps back a tick and branches off a new future on any edit. "Stop at" rules halt a run at a chosen tick or threshold, and Largest-Triangle-Three-Buckets downsampling keeps about 2,000 points per chart line. Common Ground has not changed since 26 September ([GitHub](https://github.com/fraferra/agents-world)). Because these projects are often AI-assisted and have 0–13 stars, sorting GitHub by stars misses them; searching by topic, language and recency works better.

Open-source game code supplies three mechanisms the plan lacks. Micropolis, the open-sourced SimCity, computes a cheap crime field ([scan.cpp](https://github.com/SimHacker/micropolis/blob/master/MicropolisCore/src/MicropolisEngine/src/scan.cpp); [simulate.cpp](https://github.com/SimHacker/micropolis/blob/master/MicropolisCore/src/MicropolisEngine/src/simulate.cpp)). Per block, crime is 128 minus land value plus population density, capped at 300, minus a police-coverage map smoothed three times, clamped to 0–250. Land value drops by 20 wherever crime exceeds 190, which feeds back into crime, and the police effect scales with funding. That few-dozen-line field would make a good Compare baseline against crime that emerges from individual dots.

Space Station 13 keeps a separate record layer of wanted statuses, running from None through Suspected, Arrest, Incarcerated and Parole to Discharged ([security.dm](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/master/code/__DEFINES/security.dm)). That is a ready-made state machine for recorded crime. Citybound turns needs weighted by time of day into ranked "problems" for each household ([households](https://github.com/citybound/citybound/blob/master/cb_simulation/src/economy/households/mod.rs); [market](https://github.com/citybound/citybound/blob/master/cb_simulation/src/economy/market/mod.rs)). A household with no problems waits 200 ticks before deciding again, and each shop's opening hours are shifted earlier by the estimated travel time. Shadows of Doubt, seen only through search summaries, plans each citizen's 4–10 daily journeys at dawn and generates witnesses from one global pass that checks which travelling citizens can see each other ([DevBlog 15](https://colepowered.com/shadows-of-doubt-devblog-15-moving-in-the-citizens/)). Micropolis is GPL v3 and Space Station 13 and Citybound are AGPL, so all three are for study and reimplementation from their descriptions, not for copying.

No JavaScript agent-based modelling library changes the decision to write a custom simulation core. Flocc, agentscape, atomic-agents, AgentMaps and the rest use one object per agent, publish no agent-count benchmarks, and never mention Web Workers or typed arrays. Flocc and agentscape are the most active of them, and Flocc gets about 368 npm downloads a month by the npm search API's count ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/flocc)). Two licence traps hide inside otherwise open explorables. Nicky Case's CC0 "crowds" bundles sound effects licensed for non-commercial use only ([README](https://github.com/ncase/crowds)). The CC BY 4.0 Complexity Explorables depend on the GPL-3.0 d3-widgets library ([listing](https://api.github.com/search/repositories?q=user:dirkbrockmann&sort=stars&order=desc&per_page=25)).

The large language-model societies are still limited by compute, and none of them studies crime. Project Sid's runs with more than 1,000 agents "exceeded the computational constraints" of its server ([report](https://github.com/altera-al/project-sid/blob/main/2024-10-31.pdf)), and OASIS spent 335,600 input tokens on a single step of 100 agents ([README](https://github.com/camel-ai/oasis)). If the plan's optional narration is ever built, hybrid projects show the safe pattern. Route every decision through one call site. Call the model asynchronously every N ticks so it never blocks the deterministic loop. Default to a deterministic mock agent ([Autopolis](https://github.com/sleuthy-sloth/autopolis)). And log every model output as an event so seeded replays stay identical ([simfile](https://github.com/noopolis/simfile)). Still unsearched or unverified are Reddit, Steam and itch.io beyond snippets, the live demos of every project above, developer postmortems for RimWorld, Frostpunk and Manor Lords, and the content of the underworld campaign.

## Real policing is 4–13 times sparser and far less certain than the plan assumes

### Real staffing is 0.23%, and theft barely responds to more of it

The plan staffs its city with police at 1–3% of the population, following the 4% police share in the NetLogo defaults of Epstein's model. The FBI's 2024 figures put US staffing at **2.3 sworn officers per 1,000 inhabitants**, or 0.23% ([FBI 2024 Quick Stats](https://hrc-prod-requests.s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/images/Reported-Crimes-in-the-Nation-Quick-Stats.pdf)). Epstein's default is therefore about 17 times reality, and the plan's range 4–13 times. City mode should default to 0.2–0.5%, or say on screen that each police dot is a patrol unit standing for several officers. Small lab-mode worlds can keep exaggerated shares if they are labelled as such, since 0.25% of 400 dots is a single officer.

Theft responds far less to police than murder does, and the effect varies by crime. An **elasticity** is the percentage change in crime for a 1% change in police. Levitt's 2002 estimates, read in full, put it at **−0.135 for larceny (theft), −0.195 for burglary and −0.452 for robbery**, none of them statistically significant at 95%, against −1.698 for auto theft and −0.914 for murder ([Levitt 2002](https://github.com/ryansafner/metricsF22/blob/main/files/readings/Levitt-2002.pdf)). Chalfin and McCrary's estimates, corrected for measurement error and seen only in search summaries, are similar: burglary −0.22, robbery −0.46, motor-vehicle theft −0.59, and property crime overall −0.17 ([Chalfin & McCrary 2018](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~jmccrary/chalfin_mccrary2018.pdf)). Sensible defaults are −0.15 for theft, −0.2 for burglary, −0.45 for robbery and −0.6 for vehicle theft. At those values **10% more police cuts theft by only about 1.4%**, and doubling police cuts it by about 10%. These numbers belong in the test suite as targets the simulation should reproduce, not in the code as multipliers.

The large effect is local. After the 1994 bombing in Buenos Aires, police guarded Jewish institutions around the clock. Car theft on the protected blocks fell 75%, with no detectable effect one or two blocks away, according to search summaries ([Di Tella & Schargrodsky](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/CSS_policeeffect.pdf)). The plan's `policeRecent` field, in which each cell remembers recent police presence as it fades, can produce both a small city-wide effect and a large, tightly local guard effect. Tests should check for each.

### About 4% of true thefts end in a clearance, a rate the plan never checks

Certainty of capture is the plan's largest calibration gap. In the FBI's 2024 data, read in full, police cleared **17.3% of larcenies, 15.2% of burglaries, 9.2% of motor-vehicle thefts and 30.4% of robberies** ([FBI 2024](https://hrc-prod-requests.s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/images/Reported-Crimes-in-the-Nation-Quick-Stats.pdf)). Data for 2025, released in August 2026 and seen only in a search summary, put property-crime clearance at 17.4% ([FBI](https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/violent-crime-falls-at-historic-rate-new-fbi-data-show)). Only part of crime reaches the police at all. Search summaries of the 2024 National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS) give reporting rates of about 25% for theft, 41% for burglary, 75% for vehicle theft and 73% for robbery ([BJS](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/criminal-victimization-2024)).

Multiplying reporting by clearance gives the chance that a crime that actually happened ends in a clearance (roughly, an arrest): **about 4% for theft, 6% for burglary, 7% for vehicle theft and 22% for robbery**. The plan already uses Epstein's formula only as offenders' perceived risk. That formula, 1 − exp(−2.3 × C/A), counts police (C) and active offenders (A) within sight. It is 0.90 when the two are equal, 0.68 at one officer per two offenders, and still 0.21 at one officer per ten. Actual arrests come from officers responding and pursuing, and the plan never checks how often that succeeds.

The fix starts with a test that **realised clearances** per true theft land at about 3–7% (robbery about 20%). Also consider damping **perceived risk**: offenders who see a 90% risk with one officer in sight are deterred far more than a real clearance chance of about 4% justifies. A designer could defend keeping the high figure because it makes deterrence visible on screen, but then it should be labelled as an exaggeration.

### Unequal reporting and patrol feedback are two different distortions

Realistic staffing also changes how recorded crime is produced. With one officer per several hundred residents, patrols witness few thefts, which implies that most recorded theft enters the records through victim reports. That points to two distortions the plan does not separate.

The first, unequal reporting, is static. In a study seen only in search summaries, reporting rates across Bogotá's 19 districts ranged from 13% to 33%. In a simulation modelled on those districts, that difference alone pulled predicted hotspots away from high-crime, low-reporting areas and toward high-reporting ones ([Akpinar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00128)). The second, patrol feedback, grows over time. It is the runaway loop Ensign and colleagues described, in which crimes that patrols detect attract more patrols, which then detect more crime in the same places.

Viewers should be able to switch each distortion on separately, or the simulation will teach that all distortion comes from patrol feedback. More police can even raise recorded crime while true crime falls: Chalfin and coauthors, in a study seen only in search summaries, found that larger forces make more low-level arrests, disproportionately of Black Americans ([Chalfin et al. 2022](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3m116366/qt3m116366.pdf)).

### The hotspot field's spreading term checks out, but its decay needs an explicit time step

The plan's hotspot field comes from Short et al.'s 2008 burglary model, and it partly passes a check. The paper itself could not be opened. A third-party implementation and search excerpts confirm that the plan's spatial term, (1 − η)·B + η·mean4(B), which spreads each cell's attractiveness B to its four neighbours, is algebraically identical to the paper's ([jjapp/shortBurglary](https://github.com/jjapp/shortBurglary)). The decay term needs an explicit time step. In the paper, attractiveness decays by a factor of (1 − ω·δt) each step, where δt is the length of a time step. A burglary happens with probability 1 − exp(−A·δt), where A is the cell's total attractiveness, and burglars appear at rate Γ and leave after striking. Every rate must therefore use the same time step. The plan's own approach is sound: it updates the field once per sim-hour and sets the decay from a half-life of one to two sim-weeks. But it also quotes ω = 1/15, a value from 2026 follow-up work rather than the 2008 paper. Applied at every hourly update, that would give a half-life of about 10 hours (ln 0.5 / ln(14/15) ≈ 10.05). The plan should state its time step and derive ω from the half-life. The third-party code's δt = 0.01 is the implementer's choice, not confirmed as the paper's.

Search summaries support Γ = 0.019, θ = 0.56 and η = 0.03 for one figure in the paper ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242451455_A_Statistical_Model_of_Criminal_Behavior)), and 2026 follow-up work uses ω = 1/15 ([arXiv 2605.17709](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17709)). But **the baseline attractiveness A0 = 1/30 could not be confirmed**, and the third-party implementation uses 0.2. A cheap unit test follows from the steady state: average attractiveness B̄ should equal θΓ/ω. With the values above and A0 = 1/30, that gives B̄ ≈ 0.16 and about 1,600 burglars on a 128 × 128 grid.

One adaptation matters here. In the paper Γ sets the burglary rate, but when any dot can offend, the agent model sets it instead. θ must then be rescaled so that B̄ stays comparable with A0, or the field ends up either invisible or saturated. The same 2026 paper adds police by generating burglars at (1 − Σ)·Γ·e^(−β·m/h²), where m is local police presence and Σ the arrest probability. That is a ready template for how police suppress the field.

### Half of crime falls on 2–6% of places, and trips need no buffer zone

Where crime falls now has numeric targets. Across cities, **50% of crime falls on 2–6% of street segments and 25% on 0.4–1.6%** ([Gill et al.](https://cebcp.org/wp-content/halloffame/Gill-etal-Testing-Concentration-Crime.pdf)). Counting everyone at risk, 5% of possible victims suffer about 60% of victimisations ([O et al. 2017](https://crimesciencejournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40163-017-0071-3)). Concentration should be measured only over windows in which crimes are not far fewer than cells, because sparse counts inflate it ([arXiv 1902.03105](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03105)). British offenders usually travel 1–2 miles, about half of them under a mile ([POP Center](https://popcenter.asu.edu/content/step-16-study-journey-crime)). In sprawling Dallas the medians are 4.2 miles for violent and 5.7 for property crime, with burglary the shortest trip and theft the longest ([Ackerman & Rossmo](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10940-014-9232-7)).

The "buffer zone", in which offenders supposedly avoid their own neighbourhood, lost its main systematic review, retracted in 2021 for an ecological fallacy ([retraction note](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40163-021-00143-y)). A two-dimensional effect makes a separate buffer unnecessary anyway. If each grid cell is weighted by exp(−d/λ), where d is distance from home, the number of cells grows with distance. Actual trips therefore follow d·exp(−d/λ), which is zero at home and has a median of about 1.68λ. A median trip of 1–2 km needs λ of about 0.6–1.2 km, drawn separately for each offender, since about half the variation in trip length lies between offenders ([Townsley & Sidebottom](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-9125.2010.00205.x)).

Displacement, where crime simply moves next door, is the exception rather than the rule. Across 574 observations in 102 studies, it appeared in 26% and the opposite, benefits spreading to nearby areas, in 27% ([Guerette & Bowers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229732660_Assessing_the_extent_of_crime_displacement_and_diffusion_of_benefits_A_review_of_situational_crime_prevention_evaluations)). For focused policing, spreading benefits outnumbered displacement 37% to 23% ([Bowers et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.4073/csr.2011.3)). A simple rule fits. A would-be offender deterred by a patrol moves to a nearby cell with probability about 0.25 and otherwise gives up, while neighbouring cells get a small reduction in risk.

### Three cited figures need correcting and one refining, recidivism most of all

Three cited figures need correcting and a fourth (Koper's optimum, 14–15 rather than 10–15 minutes) needs refining. Chalfin and McCrary's murder elasticity is −0.67 ± 0.47, not ± 0.48. Koper's patrol-stop study puts the chance of crime within 30 minutes of a patrol leaving at 15% (some sources say 16%) after a drive-by, against 4% after a stop of 10–16 minutes, with the best stop at 14–15 minutes ([OJP](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/just-enough-police-presence-reducing-crime-and-disorderly-behavior)). Raphael and Winter-Ebmer's "2.8–5% more property crime per percentage point of unemployment" could not be confirmed. Summaries give 1–5% across specifications ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=242571)), and Levitt's city data imply 1.0–1.2%, so 1–3% is the better central target.

The most consequential correction is to recidivism. The Bureau of Justice Statistics figure of 22% in year ten is the share of the whole released group arrested during that year, not the risk of re-offending for those not yet re-arrested ([BJS](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/recidivism-prisoners-released-24-states-2008-10-year-follow-period-2008-2018)). The model should instead match the cumulative share ever re-arrested: 43% by year one, 66% by year three and 82% by year ten. The plan's formula, a declining risk h0·e^(−t/τ) plus a constant floor h∞, can match these cumulative targets (h0 ≈ 0.68 a year, τ ≈ 1.46 years, h∞ ≈ 0.073 a year), but its constant floor eventually re-arrests everyone. A quick fit for this report found that a Weibull curve in which 15% are never re-arrested (shape 0.68, scale 1.65 years) also fits all three points, within 0.3 percentage points, and levels off. ### Deep cuts can still tip the city, and most rates await verification

Small effects at the margin do not rule out cliffs. Fonoberova and colleagues' crime model, checked against data from 5,660 US cities and seen only in search summaries, found that cutting officers below a critical level rapidly increases crime and violence ([JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/15/1/2.html)). ndouglas/SugarScape shows an analogous cliff in Epstein's rebellion model, where its police-reduction run "tips every one of 20 seeds into rebellion" ([GitHub](https://github.com/ndouglas/SugarScape)). The police slider should therefore be nearly flat around its default and steep at very low staffing, and the tests should probe both regimes.

Only three primary documents could be opened for this part of the research: the FBI's 2024 tables, Levitt 2002 and the third-party Short code. NCVS reporting rates by crime type, FBI clearance by type for 2025, and reporting rates for England and Wales remain unverified. So do Short et al.'s A0 and time step, travel distances by crime type from one consistent multi-city source, and any measured buffer radius. No causal estimate opened for this research is significant for theft: Levitt's instrumental-variable interval for larceny runs from −0.52 to +0.25. His fixed-effects estimate (−0.136, SE 0.058) is significant but not causal, and Mello (2019), seen only in a search summary, reports significant larceny effects without an elasticity captured here.

## Lengnick's economy needs re-anchoring to labour flows, retail margins and money velocity

### Two parameters are missing, and the starting values need a stated rule

The plan's economy follows Lengnick's 2013 model. Its parameters match the paper's Table 1, as written into the MIT-licensed replication's conformance tests, with two gaps ([Lengnick replication](https://github.com/avakeeling199/Lengnick-replication)). The plan omits **ξ = 0.01**: a household switches to a cheaper firm only if that firm's price is at least 1% lower. And it folds two separate probabilities into one: ψ_price = 0.25 (switch to a cheaper shop) and **ψ_quant = 0.25** (drop a shop that could not meet demand). These govern how quickly households reshuffle between shops, so they shape market concentration and firm sizes. The paper runs 6,000 months after a 1,000-month burn-in and does not specify starting values. As the plan already notes, the household cash of 3,100, price of 25 and wage of 1,428 it lists are the replication's own choices.

An independent AGPL replication lists about twenty points where the paper is ambiguous ([newwayland/baseline-economy](https://github.com/newwayland/baseline-economy)). Several matter for an integer-cent ledger. Buying in whole units makes one consumption equation redundant. The model assumes money and goods are infinitely divisible. The formulas for marginal cost and average price, and the order in which monthly profits are paid out, are not given. And households can only buy what was produced the previous day.

The plan should write down its choices: round buffers up and purchases down, set a price floor of one cent, and pay wages before working out profit shares. It should also start prices inside the markup band, between 1.025 and 1.15 times marginal cost. Marginal cost here is w/63, the monthly wage spread over 21 working days at 3 goods per worker-day. The replication's starting price of 25, against a marginal cost of 22.67, is a markup of 1.10 and sits inside the band. A JavaScript variant starts at a markup of 1.21, outside the band, so its rules spend the first months dragging prices down ([YudiWang/Baseline-Economy](https://github.com/YudiWang/Baseline-Economy)).

### Prices should change in 9–12% of months, and wages almost never fall

Lengnick's θ = 0.75 is not how often prices change. It is the probability of adopting a change once a firm's stock has drifted outside its target range, so the right test is how often prices change in practice. The replication's figure script reports a median of 9% of months for Lengnick's own model. That matches US data before 2020, when regular prices changed in 9–12% of months, or about 20% counting sales ([Nakamura & Steinsson](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v123y2008i4p1415-1464..html)). During the 2021–22 inflation the frequency roughly doubled to about 20% a month, then fell below 15% in 2023, while the typical size of each change moved little ([Montag & Villar, FEDS Note](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/price-setting-during-the-covid-era-20230829.html)). So keep the step size ϑ fixed across policy regimes and let the frequency carry inflation.

Wages almost never fall. Only about 2% of people who stay in their job get a cut in base pay in a given year, and about 35% get no change ([Grigsby, Hurst & Yildirmaz](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25628)). In August 2026, 12.4% of workers had roughly zero wage growth over the year. Wages grew 3.6% for those who stayed in their job and 5.0% for those who switched ([Atlanta Fed](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/data/wage-growth-tracker)). Lengnick lets a firm cut wages only after 24 months fully staffed, which should produce a cut rate near 2%; a rate above 10% means the rule is misbehaving.

### Lengnick's search rules need checking against US hiring and quit rates

The labour market is where Lengnick's search rules most need checking against the data. In August 2026, US hires ran at 3.3% a month, quits at 1.9%, layoffs at 1.0% and job openings at 4.3% ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/09/29/august-2026-jolts-report/)). About 1.86 million of roughly 7 million unemployed people found jobs between August and September, a **job-finding rate of about 26% a month** ([BLS flows](https://www.bls.gov/web/empsit/cps_flows_current.htm)). These numbers fit together. With unemployment at 4.1%, a stable labour market needs about 1.1% of workers to lose their jobs each month, which matches the layoff rate.

Lengnick's unemployed visit up to β = 5 firms a month. That reaches a 26% job-finding rate only if just 6% of visits find a vacancy the worker will accept. With openings at US levels across 100 firms, nearly every job seeker would be hired within the month, unless each worker's lowest acceptable wage screens out most offers. If only about 6% of firm visits find an acceptable vacancy, the plan's on-the-job search probability π = 0.1 gives job-to-job moves of about 0.3% a month (up to 2% if vacancies are plentiful), against a 1.9% quit rate. In that case π should rise to about 0.3–0.6, or employed searchers should sample several firms.

How long unemployment lasts is the hardest target. A constant 26% monthly chance of finding a job gives an average spell of 3.8 months (about 17 weeks), with 15% of the unemployed out for over 27 weeks. The observed average is 24.8 weeks, with 27% out that long ([BLS Table A-12](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.t12.htm)). In Lengnick's model the lowest acceptable wage falls 10% for each month of unemployment, which makes long spells rarer, not more common. The city therefore needs workers who differ, in skills or in stigma, and the plan's post-jail hiring stigma could supply the latter. Logging the share of firm visits that find an acceptable vacancy turns all of this into one diagnosable number.

### Shops need retail margins, not Lengnick's 2–13%

Business statistics supply the firm targets. About 78% of new US business establishments survive one year, 51% five years and 35% ten ([BLS TED](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm); [aggregator](https://startbusinessbystate.com/business-survival-rates-by-state/)). Firms exit at about 7.5% a year (establishments 8.5%), about 0.65% a month ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/business-entry-and-exit-in-the-covid-19-pandemic-a-preliminary-look-at-official-data-20220506.html)). Firm sizes follow Zipf's law, in which a few very large firms coexist with many small ones, with an exponent of about 1 ([Axtell 2001](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1062081)). With only 100 firms that exponent cannot be estimated reliably, so use Lengnick's reported size skewness of about 1.9 instead.

The shops are the real mismatch. Lengnick's markup band of 1.025–1.15 over labour cost implies gross margins of only 2.4–13%. His stock target of 0.25–1.0 months of demand sits below US retail's 1.27 months of sales in stock ([Census MTIS](https://www.census.gov/mtis/www/data/pdf/mtis_current.pdf)). US retail gross margins run 26–33% ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/margin.html)), a markup of 1.36–1.50 over what the goods cost. The answer is two presets. An exact Lengnick replication preset serves tests that compare against other implementations. A city preset has shops mark up their wholesale cost by 1.36–1.50 and hold about 0.8–1.6 months of stock.

### Fixed money pins average saving at zero

For household budgets, the 2024 Consumer Expenditure Survey gives spending shares of 33.4% on housing, 17.0% on transport and 12.9% on food ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/cesan.pdf)). The plan's Stone–Geary budgets cover a fixed subsistence amount first, then split the rest by fixed shares. Setting subsistence at the welfare floor, and choosing the shares so that average spending at mean income matches these figures, makes food take a bigger share of poorer households' budgets, as Engel's law requires. Inequality targets for 2025 are a household income Gini of 0.490 before tax and 0.448 after ([Census P60-289](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/p60-289.pdf)). For wealth, the targets are a Gini of 0.83 in the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/us-wealth-inequality-2022-modest-reversal-top-persistent-challenges-below)) and a top-1% share of 31.6% ([FRED DFA](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134)).

Two targets clash with the plan's fixed-money setting. A sustained household saving rate like the 4.1% of August 2026 ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-august-2026)) cannot persist in a stationary fixed-money economy, where aggregate household saving must average zero. It belongs only in the setting where money is issued; with fixed money, zero aggregate saving becomes a ledger test. Velocity, how often money changes hands, is the second clash. M2 velocity of 1.418 a year ([FRED M2V](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/M2V)) implies money holdings equal about 8.5 months of spending. The replication's starting cash equals about 2.2 months of wages, a velocity near 5, which is closer to everyday spending money. Neither choice is wrong, but the plan should pick one deliberately and chart velocity.

### Mark-0 predicts residual unemployment for a ledger without overdrafts

Mark-0 is a small model of firms and households with an MIT-licensed reference implementation ([KarlNaumann/Mark0](https://github.com/KarlNaumann/Mark0)). It has four phases: full employment, full unemployment, residual unemployment and self-generated crises. Two numbers decide the phase ([arXiv 1307.5319](https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.5319)). R is how fast firms hire relative to how fast they fire. Θ is how many payrolls of debt a firm can carry before going bankrupt. The research team compiled the reference code and swept both numbers in two configurations. In the team's 2015-like configuration, the economy collapsed to full unemployment when R was below about 0.6–0.7. With R at about 0.9 or above, Θ of 1 or less gave residual unemployment, Θ = 2 self-generated crises, and Θ of 3 or more full employment at about 0.4% unemployment. With the reference code's defaults, Θ ≥ 2 gave full employment and no setting produced crises.

That has a design consequence. The plan's ledger forbids negative balances, which amounts to Θ = 0, so the city should settle into residual unemployment with no self-generated crises. If business cycles prove too mild to drive the loop from poverty to crime, one remedy to try is a credit line for firms, exposed as a slider. A credit line of about two payrolls may create cycles or remove them, so test it against both configurations' phase tables.

BAM, a standard benchmark macroeconomic model, ships numeric targets in the MIT-licensed bam-engine ([bam-engine](https://github.com/kganitis/bam-engine)). Unemployment should average 6.5% ± 2.5 points, with anything above 20% counting as collapse, and vacancies should run at about 13%. The model also targets the Phillips, Okun and Beveridge correlations, which link unemployment to inflation, output and vacancies, at about −0.10, −0.85 and −0.27. These are tighter and more meaningful than the plan's current exit bands of 1–40% unemployment and prices within a factor of four.

On licences, the Jamel and JMAB macro models are GPL-3.0 and the newwayland replication is AGPL-3.0. The Caiani benchmark and the K+S port have no licence file, so all of these are for study only. BeforeIT.jl is a conflict: its LICENSE file says Apache-2.0 while its paper says AGPL-3.0 ([BeforeIT.jl](https://github.com/bancaditalia/BeforeIT.jl)).

### Moderate policies should move outcomes gently

The policy sliders need gentler expectations. Across 138 US state minimum-wage increases, the number of low-wage jobs stayed essentially unchanged over five years, with no job loss in retail and restaurants ([Cengiz et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434)). A moderate floor should therefore move employment by about ±1% and compress the bottom of the wage distribution. Job losses should start only once the floor exceeds what the weakest shops can pay.

The default wealth tax of 0.25% a month compounds to about 3% a year, which is aggressive by historical standards. Denmark's reforms showed taxable wealth at the top responding strongly, but those estimates include avoidance that the simulation cannot model ([Jakobsen et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24371)). A guaranteed $1,000 a month cut recipients' labour-force participation by about 2 percentage points and their hours by about 1.3 a week in the 2024 version of the OpenResearch study; a later revision reports 4.2 points ([NBER w32719](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)). So the welfare slider should cut participation by a few points, not collapse employment.

## Twenty seeds certify only large effects, so verdicts need power and a third outcome

### Twenty seeds detect only effects beyond "large"

The plan judges each behavioural claim across 20 random seeds and labels it Holds or Fails. The research team's power simulations show what that can establish. They measure effects with the **Vargha–Delaney A**, the probability that a run under one setting produces a higher value than a run under the other. A = 0.5 means no effect. Its standard thresholds of 0.56, 0.64 and 0.71 for small, medium and large effects ([Vargha–Delaney code](https://gist.github.com/timm/5622240)) correspond, to two decimals and assuming normally distributed outcomes, to Cohen's familiar d of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8.

| Seeds per arm | Smallest effect detectable (A, at 1% significance and 80% power) | Lowest pass rate consistent with every seed passing (95% confidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.81 (beyond "large") | 0.86 |
| 50 | 0.70 | 0.94 |
| 100 | 0.64 (medium) | 0.97 |
| ≈540 | 0.56 (small) | — |

So 20 seeds per arm detect only effects bigger than "large", while medium effects need 100–130 seeds and small ones 360–690. For properties checked seed by seed, 20 passes out of 20 shows only that the pass rate is at least 0.86; showing 0.95 takes 59 out of 59. Showing that something has *no* meaningful effect takes an equivalence test with about 180 seeds per arm. A claim that misses significance at 20 seeds has therefore not been shown to fail; it is **Inconclusive**. Realistic calibration makes this worse. At a theft elasticity of −0.15, adding 10% more police is a small effect that may need hundreds of seeds to certify, depending on how much theft varies between seeds, which should be measured on a pilot batch. Certified claims should compare large contrasts instead, such as tripling police, which cuts theft by about 15%.

### Three verdicts, judged by claim type

The research proposes a procedure whose statistics code (a Mann–Whitney test, the A measure and a bootstrap interval) fits in about 60 lines of TypeScript. Each claim is first tagged as one of three types: an **estimate** ("random exchange gives a Gini of 0.5"), a **per-seed property** ("prices stay finite in every run") or a **comparison** ("more police means less theft"). Each type is then judged its own way.

Comparisons run on the same seed list in both arms, which halves the seeds needed when the paired results correlate at 0.5, and use a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired differences. A comparison **Holds** when p < 0.01, the effect size is at least 0.64 and the direction matches the prediction. It **Fails** only when the effect is significantly reversed, and is otherwise Inconclusive. Per-seed properties use Wald's sequential test, which stops as soon as the evidence suffices: testing a 95% pass rate against 85% at 5% error, it accepts after 27 straight passes and rejects after three straight failures. Estimates pass only if their confidence interval lies inside the target plus or minus a stated margin. That is the equivalence logic Axtell, Axelrod, Epstein and Cohen proposed in 1996 ([SFI working paper](https://sfi-edu.s3.amazonaws.com/sfi-edu/production/uploads/sfi-com/dev/uploads/filer/08/f5/08f53c29-81ee-4709-8d2e-0e134bb11700/95-07-065.pdf)).

A two-stage version keeps 20 seeds for speed. It declares Holds at once if p < 0.001 with 20 seeds per arm, stops if the effect size is below 0.55, and otherwise extends to 50. Beyond about 20 claims, Holm's correction for multiple tests applies. Fixed seed lists in Vitest keep verdicts from flickering between runs, and a nightly re-judgement on fresh seeds catches verdicts that only hold for the fixed list.

ndouglas/SugarScape adds a lesson about choosing claims. Audits of its watching model found that three of five judged results could be predicted straight from the rules, "so they weren't tests" ([GitHub](https://github.com/ndouglas/SugarScape)). On tooling, the `wilcoxonRankSum` function in `simple-statistics` returns only the rank sum, not a p-value ([source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/simple-statistics/simple-statistics/main/src/wilcoxon_rank_sum.js)), so the tests should use `@stdlib/stats-wilcoxon` for the signed-rank test, checked once against SciPy.

### One-at-a-time sweeps stay inside a ball that fills 0.25% of the parameter space

The plan's sweeps vary one or two sliders on a grid around a base point, so they measure local slopes and miss interactions among the other parameters, such as "police matter only when thieves are many and wages are low". Every one-at-a-time test point lies inside the ball that fits within the space of possible settings. By the team's computation, that ball fills only 0.25% of the space when there are ten parameters.

The replacement has two stages. First, Morris screening: 20 trajectories through 20 parameters need 420 design points. At 10 seeds each that is 4,200 runs, under two hours on six worker threads even at 10 seconds per run. Second, Sobol sensitivity indices on the 5–8 parameters that survive screening. No maintained library for this exists on npm. The practical route is to exchange text files with SALib, a Python library that documents exactly that workflow for models not written in Python ([SALib docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SALib/SALib/main/docs/user_guide/basics.rst)). The budget depends on how long one headless run takes. A full Sobol analysis across 20 parameters is practical only if a run takes 0.5 seconds or less, so run time should be measured at M2.

### Patterns, not datasets, calibrate the city

Calibration needs no real dataset. Pattern-oriented modelling treats the crime and economy targets above as filters ([Grimm et al. 2005](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1116681); search summary). The method has four steps. Choose 4–8 patterns at different levels, each with a numeric band. Run one Latin hypercube sweep, a spread-out sample of 2,000–5,000 parameter settings, at five seeds each. Keep the settings that meet every pattern. And hold one or two patterns back to check afterwards. A healthy filter passes 5–10% of points; if fewer than 0.5% pass, check the model's structure before tuning ranges. The same sweep feeds the sensitivity indices, so one batch of runs serves both purposes. Heavier tools are worth it only if runs take seconds. One is the Bank of Italy's Black-it, which fits a model by matching simulated statistics ([Black-it](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bancaditalia/black-it/main/README.md)). Another is history matching followed by approximate Bayesian computation ([McCulloch et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/185400/); search summary).

### Metamorphic tests turn transformations into exact checks

Metamorphic relations stand in for exact answers where none exist: each transforms the input and states how the output must change ([NIST](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=932547)). Many become exact fingerprint tests if every random draw is tied to a stable dot ID rather than to the dot's position in an array. That means changing the plan's random streams, which are currently organised per subsystem only. Four relations are especially useful. **Multiplying every money amount by 100** should leave goods, jobs, thefts and the Gini identical while money outputs scale exactly, which catches hard-coded constants such as "steal 5". **Shuffling the order in which dots are stored** should leave the ID-sorted fingerprint unchanged. If it changes, the order in which dots act matters, the artefact that broke one of twelve comparisons when Axtell and colleagues checked Sugarscape against Axelrod's culture model. **Scheduling a policy change of zero size** should leave the run bit-identical, which catches the most common hidden bug: a code path that should do nothing but still consumes random draws. And **removing all thieves** must zero thefts and theft arrests on every tick.

Comparing the arrest submodule against Mesa's implementation of Epstein's model completes the picture. Check three standards: numerical identity, the same distribution of results, and the same direction of effects. The 1996 comparison of Sugarscape against Axelrod's model took about 60 person-hours.

### Measure the burn-in, and report regimes as odds

The burn-in should be measured, not assumed. MSER-5, which Hoad and colleagues selected from 44 warm-up methods, picks the cut-off point from series averaged over five seeds in about 20 lines of code ([Hoad et al.](https://higherlogicdownload.s3.amazonaws.com/INFORMS/ef27cc87-0593-4a9b-85b3-52b5bbeae306/UploadedImages/paper77-81.pdf)). By the team's own rule of thumb, not Hoad and colleagues', its answer should be multiplied by 1.5, and the test should fail if the cut-off falls in the second half of the run.

Next, compare averages over time within one long run against values across 50 seeds. If they differ, the model has distinct regimes, and the dashboard should report something like "collapses in 7 of 50 seeds" rather than an average.

An in-app gauge that watches variance and autocorrelation rise can warn of an approaching tipping point, as in the Dakos early-warning toolbox ([earlywarnings](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/earlywarningtoolbox/earlywarnings-R/master/R/generic_ews.R)). Such signals also raise false alarms ([Boettiger & Hastings](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.1204)). So the gauge should be calibrated to at most 5% false alarms on stable presets and tested on a run where police are steadily reduced. It should read "fragility rising", not "collapse predicted".

### ODD+D and TRACE map onto the test plan

ODD, the standard protocol for describing agent-based models, opens its 2020 update with "Purpose and patterns", which ties the documentation to the claim tests ([JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/23/2/7.html)). ODD+D adds sections on how agents make decisions and on theoretical background, which suits dots that choose whether to steal ([Müller et al.](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/usys/ites/ecosystem-management-dam/documents/EducationDOC/EM_DOC/Recommended%20readingDOC/Muller_2013.pdf)). TRACE organises a modelling notebook into eight elements, which map onto the planned invariants, known-answer tests, sensitivity runs and held-out patterns ([TRACE](https://www2.econ.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/TRACE.ModFramework.GrimmEtAl2014.pdf)). The CoMSES model library offers peer review, a badge and a citable DOI to runnable models with ODD-style documentation ([CoMSES](https://www.comses.net/reviews/)). These four documentation sources were seen only in search summaries. One caveat applies to all the power figures above: they assume normally distributed differences. Counts with many zeros, such as arrests, behave differently, so the power calculation should be re-run on a pilot batch of real runs. No published evaluation of early-warning signals in crime or market simulations was found.

## Chrome 150 broke Math-based replay, and hidden desktop tabs do not throttle the worker

### Chrome 150 changed Math results, while @stdlib stayed bit-identical

The plan keeps most transcendental functions out of the core, except the power function in Lengnick's consumption rule, and promises only that a run replays identically on the same browser engine. That promise is no longer reliable. The JavaScript standard fixes exactly how + − × ÷ and square root round. But it leaves sin, cos, exp, log, pow, atan2, hypot and the `**` operator "implementation-approximated", so each engine may return slightly different results ([ECMAScript spec](https://github.com/tc39/ecma262/blob/main/spec.html)). V8 15.0, the engine that shipped in Chrome 150 on 30 June 2026, now calls LLVM libc for exp, log, atan2, tan and other functions. That library is correctly rounded for exp and log; atan2 is documented to within 1 ulp (one unit in the last place), though no misrounding appeared in 100,000 inputs. Its `Math.pow` calls the operating system's own pow, which differs from one OS to another ([V8 15.0 ieee754.cc](https://github.com/v8/v8/blob/15.0-lkgr/src/base/ieee754.cc); [flags](https://github.com/v8/v8/blob/15.0-lkgr/src/flags/flag-definitions.h)). Firefox uses fdlibm for most functions but the operating system's library for sin, cos, tan and pow ([Math.cpp](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/js/src/builtin/Math.cpp)). Safari's JavaScriptCore calls C-library functions, which on Apple devices come from Apple's own library ([MathObject.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/JavaScriptCore/runtime/MathObject.cpp)). The research team measured the consequences on 100,000 inputs per function, using Node 22 (V8 12.4), Deno 2.9.7 (V8 15.0) and Bun 1.3.14 (JavaScriptCore). The table shows the share of inputs on which two engines returned different bits.

| Function | V8 12.4 vs JavaScriptCore | V8 15.0 vs JavaScriptCore | V8 15.0 vs V8 12.4 | @stdlib, all three engines |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sin | 3.38% | 0.12% | 3.41% | 0 |
| exp | 9.59% | 0.07% | 9.60% | 0 |
| log | 1.67% | 0.01% | 1.67% | 0 |
| pow | 9.74% | 0.00% | 9.74% | 0 |
| atan2 | 17.77% | 0.09% | 17.78% | 0 |
| hypot | 35.56% | 35.56% | 0.00% | 0 |

Chrome 150 now agrees far more often with JavaScriptCore on Linux's C library (as run by Bun) than older V8 did, but it disagrees with its own predecessor, so a replay recorded before June 2026 diverges after it. Safari on Apple devices uses Apple's own library, which was not tested. The @stdlib packages are pure-JavaScript versions of the FreeBSD and Go math libraries ([stdlib exp](https://github.com/stdlib-js/math-base-special-exp)). They returned identical bits on all 800,000 inputs across the three engines, at 1.0–1.7 times the cost of `Math.*` for sin, cos, atan2 and hypot, 1.4–3.4 times for exp and log, and 3–10 times for pow. A 4,096-entry sine lookup table built with @stdlib was also bit-identical. It ran at about 16 ns a call against 37–46 ns for library sine, with a maximum error of 3 × 10⁻⁷.

The fix has three parts. First, a lint rule banning Math's transcendental functions and `**` in the simulation core. Second, a deterministic-math module: @stdlib-built lookup tables for code that runs per agent per tick, and direct @stdlib calls elsewhere, including the power function in Lengnick's consumption rule, (m/P̄)^0.9. Third, replay-hash tests on Node, Bun and Deno and in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit. The payoff is an upgrade rather than a repair, because share links should then replay identically on any conforming engine, which CI must confirm for Firefox, ARM64 and Apple's library. Firefox's SpiderMonkey engine, ARM64 phones and Apple's math library were not tested.

### Workers run flat out in hidden desktop tabs

The plan's simulation loop has no answer for hidden tabs, and that matters more than expected. In the source code of all three shipping engines, a dedicated worker's own timers keep running at full speed when its page is hidden. Chromium's worker-throttling feature is disabled by default ([worker_thread_scheduler.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/scheduler/worker/worker_thread_scheduler.cc); [features.h](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/scheduler/common/features.h)). WebKit's timer code says "We don't throttle timers in worker threads" ([DOMTimer.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/page/DOMTimer.cpp)). Firefox throttles workers only in its Nightly builds ([StaticPrefList.yaml](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/modules/libpref/init/StaticPrefList.yaml)). Meanwhile, in a background Chrome tab, chained timers on the main thread drop to one wake-up a minute after 60 seconds, and requestAnimationFrame slows or stops. A simulation left in a background desktop tab will therefore keep a CPU core busy and drain a laptop battery. Mobile browsers freeze or suspend background pages outright, which can kill the worker mid-run.

The worker should pause when `visibilitychange` reports the page hidden, save a tick-indexed checkpoint on `pagehide`, and resume by resetting its time accumulator rather than running the missed ticks. It should yield through a MessageChannel, because `setTimeout(4)` inside a worker still hits a 4 ms minimum delay. Determinism is unaffected, because state depends on the tick count and logged inputs, never on wall-clock time. Playwright, the browser-testing tool, launches Chromium with background throttling disabled ([chromiumSwitches.ts](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/blob/main/packages/playwright-core/src/server/chromium/chromiumSwitches.ts)), so tests must fake visibility changes rather than rely on real browser behaviour.

### Phones get 10,000 dots by default and earn more on a start-up check

No public particle-count benchmarks for phones could be retrieved, so phone limits come from browser source code. WebKit caps canvas area at 8192 × 8192 device pixels on iOS ([CanvasBase.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/html/CanvasBase.cpp)). It allows 16 active WebGL contexts on the main thread and 4 per worker before discarding the oldest ([WebGLRenderingContextBase.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/html/canvas/WebGLRenderingContextBase.cpp)). And it halves animation to 30 frames a second in Low Power Mode or when the phone overheats ([AnimationFrameRate.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/platform/graphics/AnimationFrameRate.cpp)).

The research therefore recommends a default of 10,000 dots on phones. 25,000 should be allowed only if a start-up check of 300 ticks at 10,000 dots finds 95% of ticks under 1.5 ms, and 100,000 should stay desktop-only. It also recommends capping the device pixel ratio at 2, which cuts pixels drawn by 56% on an iPhone that reports 3, and updating charts at most four times a second. If tick time rises by half for ten seconds, a sign the phone is overheating, the app should drop one tier. That replaces the plan's M0 exit test of "10,000 dots at 60 fps", which Safari may refuse to deliver however good the code is.

### The plan should settle on a 1 KB WebGL2 renderer rather than 110–157 KB of PixiJS

The research measured bundle sizes with esbuild, because the usual size-checking websites were blocked. PixiJS 8.22.0 costs about **265 KB gzip** in full, 157 KB for its auto-detecting `Application` plus ParticleContainer, and 110 KB for a WebGL-only renderer plus ParticleContainer ([PixiJS](https://github.com/pixijs/pixijs)). A hand-written WebGL2 renderer that draws every dot in one instanced call, including context-loss handling, is about **1.1 KB**. An issue opened on 23 September 2026 reports that several PixiJS 8.21 pages open in separate iOS Safari tabs can lose their graphics contexts for good. The result is "a permanent black screen or, in some cases, a complete page crash" ([pixijs#12224](https://github.com/pixijs/pixijs/issues/12224)).

Since the city draws only dots, the plan should settle on the raw WebGL2 renderer its M0 already specifies, behind a narrow `DotRenderer` interface, so PixiJS can come back if text or sprites are ever needed. That brings the library budget, with uPlot, Comlink and lil-gui, to about 34 KB gzip, against about 144 KB with PixiJS. The renderer should use one WebGL context per page. It should call `preventDefault()` on `webglcontextlost`, which the WebGL spec requires before a context can be restored ([WebGL spec](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/WebGL/blob/main/specs/latest/1.0/index.html)). When the context returns, it should rebuild its two shaders and three buffers while the simulation keeps running. It should release the context on `pagehide`, and fall back to Canvas2D, capped at about 5,000 dots, when WebGL2 is unavailable.

Moving rendering off the main thread is possible but costly. WebGL2 in an OffscreenCanvas needs Safari 17. Safari does not support `devicePixelContentBoxSize`, or 2D context-loss events for OffscreenCanvas, and how it handles WebGL context loss there is undocumented ([MDN browser-compat-data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data)). uPlot also needs the page's DOM ([uPlot](https://github.com/leeoniya/uPlot)). So rendering should stay on the main thread up to 25,000 dots, with a separate render worker only for the desktop tier of 100,000. It should not run inside the simulation worker, except possibly for a tiny embed build, because shader compiles and graphics-driver stalls there would delay ticks.

### CI must compare replay hashes across three browsers

Continuous-integration tests need three layers ([Vitest browser mode](https://github.com/vitest-dev/vitest/blob/main/docs/guide/browser/index.md)). The first is the pure simulation core, run in Node, Bun and Deno against golden hashes. The second is worker protocol tests, simulated in one thread with `@vitest/web-worker` or run in real browsers with Vitest 5's browser mode. The third is Playwright end-to-end runs inside the Playwright Docker image, in Chromium with software WebGL (`--use-angle=swiftshader --enable-unsafe-swiftshader`), Firefox and WebKit ([Chromium gl_switches](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/ui/gl/gl_switches.cc)).

The tests that matter for this architecture make four checks. The replay hash after 2,000 ticks must be identical in all three browsers and match the Node golden. A forced context loss and restore through `WEBGL_lose_context` must leave a frame that is not blank. The worker's tick counter must stop and resume without catching up when visibility is faked. And statistics of the canvas pixels must stay within tolerance, rather than matching an exact screenshot, because software WebGL draws differently from a real GPU.

### Role needs a shape as well as a colour

WCAG 2.2, the web accessibility standard, sets four requirements at Level A. Every control must work from a keyboard ([2.1.1](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/keyboard.html)). There must be a way to pause content that moves for more than five seconds, and to pause or slow charts that update on their own ([2.2.2](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/pause-stop-hide.html)). There must be no more than three flashes a second ([2.3.1](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)). And colour must never be the only way information is shown ([1.4.1](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/use-of-color.html)). At Level AA it adds 3:1 contrast for graphics needed to understand the content ([1.4.11](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/21/non-text-contrast.html)), and status messages that screen readers can announce without moving focus ([4.1.3](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/21/status-messages.html)).

The plan's "role is hue" rule fails 1.4.1, so each role also needs a shape. Circle, square, triangle, diamond and ring are cheap to draw in the shader, chosen by one byte per dot. The team simulated colour blindness on candidate palettes. For four roles, Paul Tol's "muted" colours #DDCC77, #117733, #88CCEE and #AA4499 stayed the most distinguishable under every type of colour blindness while passing 3:1 contrast on a dark background ([tol-colors](https://pypi.org/project/tol-colors/)). Tol's #332288 and #882255, and Petroff's #7A21DD, fail contrast on dark backgrounds.

The app should also start paused when the user has asked for reduced motion ([Media Queries 5](https://github.com/w3c/csswg-drafts/blob/main/mediaqueries-5/Overview.bs)). Play/Pause should come first in the keyboard tab order, with keyboard shortcuts. A short, polite summary should be announced to screen readers at most every ten seconds. And each chart should get a text label and a data table, since uPlot has had an open accessibility issue with no response since May 2024 ([uPlot#954](https://github.com/leeoniya/uPlot/issues/954)). Real-device performance is the largest unknown in this part of the research. Chromium's numeric limit on WebGL contexts, whether GitHub-hosted CI runners have GPUs, how frame times compare between main-thread and worker rendering, and which PixiJS features work inside a worker are also unestablished.

## Bets before runs, bias in records not people, and an honest list of omissions

### Predicting first is what makes watching teach

The strongest learning evidence, seen only in search summaries, says that watching a demonstration teaches little on its own. In Crouch and colleagues' physics classes, students who passively watched a demonstration understood the concept no better than students who never saw it. Students who first predicted the outcome understood significantly more ([Mazur group](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment)). PhET's more than 275 think-aloud interviews found simulations effective only when the student's own questions drove the interaction ([PER-Central](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=12269)).

Nicky Case's playtested rules point the same way. Start with a concrete experience, climb toward abstraction one surprising "BUT" at a time, and save the full sandbox for the end ([Case](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/how-i-make-an-explorable-explanation.md)). Hold content back once playtesters start skimming, as Earth: A Primer did ([Case 2014 notes](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/explorable-explanations.md)). Make readers place a bet before revealing the answer ([Case 2018](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/explorable-explanations-4-more-design-patterns.md)). His notes on Neurotic Neurons add that toggling rules on and off "just made it more confusing", and that he hand-built its network so every element was worth clicking ([Case 2015](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/neurotic-neurons-design-patterns.md)). The Evolution of Trust renamed the strategy Tit for Tat to "Copycat", so players who knew the name would not bet on it out of recognition ([Trust notes](https://github.com/ncase/trust/blob/gh-pages/notes/index.html)).

For the Dot Society, the plan's rules cards should become **bet cards** that lock in a prediction before Run. The first seeds should be hand-picked, and labelled as hand-picked, so the first run shows the effect, and each city-mode slider should unlock only after the lab card that introduces it. Policies should carry neutral names such as "Hotspot patrol" rather than "Biased predictor". The plan's `arrestRule` switch belongs in tests and an expert panel, not in the main interface.

### Small multiples for analysis, animation for the hook

For analysis, animation is the weakest view, according to two studies seen only in search summaries. Robertson and colleagues found animation slower than small multiples or motion trails, and less accurate than small multiples, although viewers found it more fun and it worked well for presentation ([IEEE TVCG](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2008.125)). Hypothetical outcome plots, which animate individual random draws one after another, gave much more accurate judgments than error bars ([Hullman et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0142444)). So the animated city stays as the hook. The comparison of true and recorded crime should run as two synced small panels rather than one map that toggles between them. Spread across seeds should appear as a flicker of outcomes or a fan of thin lines, with a caption such as "in 87 of 100 runs".

Colony-simulation games offer interface patterns, all seen only in search summaries. RimWorld colours its event letters blue, grey, yellow or red by severity ([RimWorld Wiki](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Events)). The police view in Cities: Skylines shades roads by police coverage and buildings by crime probability on one map ([wiki](https://skylines.paradoxwikis.com/Info_views)). Frostpunk's hope and discontent meters can both be high at once ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/)). And Crusader Kings III's nested tooltips, which Victoria 3 adopted so that "you don't need an econ degree", explain any number on hover ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/victoria-3/nested-tooltip-system)). Applied here, these suggest an event feed for thefts, arrests and bankruptcies, coloured by severity, and one map that shades cells by true crime and marks recorded incidents on top. They also suggest two independent meters, such as fear of crime and trust in police, and money balances that expand into money in and money out by counterparty.

### The headline card: identical crime, different reporting

The published critiques of predictive policing, seen only in search summaries apart from Ensign and colleagues' code, describe exactly the mechanism the plan wants to show. In Oakland, estimated drug use was roughly the same across the city, yet drug arrests in two areas were about 200 times higher than elsewhere. A PredPol-style algorithm trained on those arrests would have targeted Black residents at roughly twice the rate of white residents ([Lum & Isaac](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2016.00960.x)). Ensign and colleagues showed that such systems send police back to the same neighbourhoods "regardless of the true crime rate". Their code models this as an urn that gains balls where incidents are found, with options for adding only reported crimes, a ready-made rule for a lab card ([runaway-feedback-loops-src](https://github.com/algofairness/runaway-feedback-loops-src)). Richardson, Schultz and Crawford documented 13 jurisdictions where predictive policing overlapped with documented unlawful or biased policing ([NYU Law Review](https://www.nyulawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/NYULawReview-94-Richardson_etal-FIN.pdf)). The Markup found that PredPol predictions across 38 cities mostly avoided whiter neighbourhoods ([The Markup 2021](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2021/12/02/crime-prediction-software-promised-to-be-free-of-biases-new-data-shows-it-perpetuates-them)). In Plainfield, New Jersey, fewer than 100 of 23,631 predictions matched a later-reported crime of the predicted type ([The Markup 2023](https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes)).

The headline lab card follows directly. Two districts have **identical true crime but reporting rates of 0.13 and 0.33**, the range found across Bogotá. A predictor is trained on recorded crime, and the reader bets, before pressing Run, on where the patrols will go. The predictor should start by sending about 2.5 times more patrols to the district that reports more, then widen the gap as those patrols detect more crime. The card uses deliberately dense, labelled patrols; at calibrated staffing the patrol-feedback step would be weak.

### Put the asymmetry in records, not in the dots

The same move that sharpens the lesson also handles the main ethical risk. Abstract dots on their own invite the standard critique of Schelling-style segregation toys. Such toys tell a symmetric story with no institutions in it, when real segregation is "a mechanism by which one group excludes the other" ([American Scientist](https://www.americanscientist.org/article/the-math-of-segregation)). The dots should therefore carry only roles and states, with no demographic attributes, names or real places. "Thief" should be a state a citizen enters and leaves under pressure, not a kind of person. The imbalance should sit where the critiques locate it: in how patrols are allocated, how crime is reported and what gets recorded. Three views should be labelled plainly: true crime ("only the simulator knows this"), recorded crime and predicted hotspots.

Earlier explorables show how to be honest about simplification. Parable of the Polygons says "we gave his model a happy ending" and links to real data ([Polygons](https://github.com/ncase/polygons/blob/gh-pages/index.html)). Case published "everything I know I lied about" for Neurotic Neurons ([simplifications](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/neurotic-neurons-simplifications.md)). And What Happens Next? criticised coverage that reported a forecast without "IF WE DO NOTHING", inviting readers to challenge its numbers with sliders ([words.md](https://github.com/ncase/covid-19/blob/master/words/words.md)). Following these precedents, the Dot Society should ship a "What this toy leaves out" page at launch, covering no courts, simplified reporting, no demographics and no history of redlining. It should put conditional wording and a "toy, not forecast" note beside every chart, expose the predictor's update rule and the reporting rates as sliders, and keep real product names out of the simulation itself. It should also include one scenario in which policing visibly cuts true crime, so the lesson is about feedback from records rather than "policing is useless". No published guidance on that last risk was found; it is the research team's own inference.

### Hits rode the news, so the omissions page ships first

Case's own figures show what a hit looks like. By his 2019 count, Parable of the Polygons (2014) reached 3 million plays and The Evolution of Trust (2017) 5 million, and he credits timing with the news: Ferguson, and a year he called "zero-trust" ([Case 2010–2019](https://github.com/ncase/blog/blob/main/src/posts/2010-2019.md)). The launch kit follows from the sources. The piece should be playable at the URL with no signup, which Hacker News's Show HN asks for ([Show HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html); search summary), and should encode the whole run in the URL, as TensorFlow Playground does ([state.ts](https://github.com/tensorflow/playground/blob/master/src/state.ts)). Each scenario needs its own 1200 × 600 preview card, like Trust's, carrying the four required Open Graph link-preview properties plus image alt text ([OGP](https://github.com/facebook/open-graph-protocol/blob/master/index.html)). The share text should carry a bet, and a 10–20-second clip of true and recorded crime diverging should be labelled as one run of many. Text should live in separate, translation-ready files, which is how Trust gathered more than 50 community translations ([Trust](https://github.com/ncase/trust)). And the piece should go to explorabl.es, whose featured picks favour free work that needs no download ([explorabl.es](https://github.com/explorableexplanations/explorableexplanations.github.io)).

Search summaries say commenters in a Hacker News thread criticised Trust's low-contrast captions and colour-blind-unfriendly colours ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=14864183)), one more reason to finish the accessibility work before launch. And because launches timed to the news draw critical readers, the omissions page must already be live.

### Permissive licences fit, and funding will be modest

Borrowed code carries different obligations. MIT code needs its copyright and permission notice kept. Apache-2.0 code needs the licence text, prominent notes on changed files, and any upstream NOTICE contents, which an in-app credits panel can display ([Apache License 2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)). CC0 material needs nothing. MIT or Apache-2.0 suits the project's code, and CC BY 4.0 or CC0 suits its prose and rules cards. The project should state which licence covers which part, since Creative Commons does not recommend its licences for software ([choosealicense](https://github.com/github/choosealicense.com/blob/gh-pages/non-software.md)). CC BY has one argument in its favour: requiring attribution keeps the "toy, not forecast" framing attached when cards are reused.

Funding will be modest. Case's Patreon "doubled" after Trust and then covered food and rent. Current snapshots, undated, show about 540–640 paying members each for Case and for Primer ([Case](https://www.patreon.com/ncase); [Primer](https://www.patreon.com/primerlearning)). The large sums come from paid editions of free works. Dwarf Fortress, according to search summaries, went from about $15,000 a month in donations to $7.23 million gross in January 2023, after its Steam release ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2023/02/02/dwarf-fortress-revenue-7-million-january-bay-12-games)). Distill's 2021 hiatus, after its editors gave some articles more than 50 hours of help and its volunteers burned out, argues for releasing lab cards one at a time ([Distill](https://github.com/distillpub/post--distill-hiatus/blob/master/index.md)). Hacker News, Reddit and YouTube reach numbers and every game-interface claim remain unverified, and no controlled study of scrubbable timelines in explorables was found.

## Conclusion

Realism and legibility now pull in opposite directions. Calibrated numbers make the city's policy effects small, local and slow: about 1.4% less theft for 10% more police, a 4% chance that a theft ends in a clearance, and hotspots that fade over weeks. Effects like these are invisible in one animated run and expensive to certify statistically. That sharpens the risk the plan already ranked highest, illegibility: calibrated effects are so small and slow that one run teaches nothing on screen. The answer is a division of labour that the plan's two modes already half-provide. Lab cards use deliberately exaggerated, labelled contrasts, such as tripled police or reporting rates of 0.13 versus 0.33, whose answers are worked out on paper and certified with paired seeds. City mode runs calibrated values and shows only distributions across seeds. Being open about where exaggeration replaces calibration is itself part of the ethical framing.

Calibration strengthens the plan's own conclusion that being verifiable, not being first, is the edge. economySim, Econ-Engine and primordial show that struct-of-arrays storage, integer-cent ledgers and seeded determinism are now commodity features, and ndouglas/SugarScape shows that rule-based theft with witnesses can appear in days. What no one has shipped is a crime-and-market society with three properties. Every on-screen claim carries a properly powered verdict. Share links replay bit-identically on any conforming engine. And the true-versus-recorded lesson locates bias in records rather than in people. All three are cheap compared with the simulation core, and all three are hard for a weekend clone to fake. The first step is also the cheapest: before M4 hard-codes anything, spend a day verifying the dozen numbers that currently rest on search summaries.
